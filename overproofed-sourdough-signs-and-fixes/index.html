Overproofed Sourdough: Signs, Fixes and How to Prevent It

Overproofed dough is flat, slack and sticky, and it’s more common than people think once a kitchen warms up or a starter gets very lively. Here’s how to spot it, rescue it and prevent it.

Quick answer

  • Signs: the dough is very puffy, slack and sticky, collapses when you turn it out or score it, spreads flat in the oven, and the crumb has a few large holes under a dense, sour bottom.
  • Before baking: do the poke test. A dent that never springs back suggests overproofing; you can gently reshape and give it a short second proof, or turn it into focaccia.
  • Next time: shorten bulk fermentation in a warm kitchen, use less starter, or put the shaped dough in the fridge sooner.
  • Warm weather and a very active starter are the usual causes. Judging the dough instead of the clock prevents most overproofing.

Overproofed or underproofed?

The two are easy to confuse because both can give a disappointing loaf. Here’s a rough guide:

  • Overproofed: flat, wide loaf; little oven spring; weak or no ear; very open, irregular holes near the top with a tighter, gummy band near the bottom; a pronounced sour or boozy smell.
  • Underproofed: tight, dense crumb with a few big tunnels; the loaf may burst open in odd places; it feels heavy for its size. See our dense and gummy sourdough guide for those fixes.

How to tell before you bake

The poke test

Flour a finger and press about half an inch into the shaped dough. If the dent springs back slowly and leaves a slight impression, it’s usually ready. If it springs back immediately, it needs more time. If it stays put or the dough sighs and deflates, it has probably gone too far. The test is less reliable for cold dough straight from the fridge, so read it alongside the other signs.

What the dough looks like

Overproofed dough often has big bubbles popping on the surface, looks shiny and slack, and loses its shape when you tip it out of the banneton. During bulk, a dough that has more than doubled and started to fall back is a warning sign.

Can you save overproofed dough?

Often, yes, at least partly:

  1. Reshape and reproof briefly. Gently degas, preshape and shape again, then proof for a shorter time (keep an eye on it; it will move fast). The result is usually decent, if not perfect.
  2. Bake it in a pan. A loaf tin holds a slack dough up so it bakes into a good sandwich loaf.
  3. Make focaccia or flatbread. Press it into an oiled tray, dimple it, and bake. An overproofed dough is great for this.
  4. Bake it anyway. A flat loaf still tastes good. Score shallowly, and get it into a very hot, covered pot quickly to give it the best chance.

How to prevent overproofing

1. Adjust for a warm kitchen

Fermentation speeds up a lot as the temperature rises. In a warm kitchen, a recipe’s bulk time can be hours too long. Cooler mixing water helps, as does checking the dough earlier than the recipe says.

2. Use less starter

Cutting the starter (for example from 20% to 10% of the flour weight) slows the whole schedule and gives you a wider window. Many bakers do this in summer.

3. Watch the rise, not the clock

Mark the starting level on a straight-sided container and end bulk when the dough has grown by roughly 50–75% and looks airy, rather than at a fixed time.

4. Cold-proof the shaped loaf

Proofing the shaped dough in the fridge overnight slows it right down and makes timing much more forgiving. It also makes scoring easier. Cover the banneton so the surface doesn’t dry out.

5. Check the temperature

Knowing your dough’s temperature tells you whether to expect a fast or slow bulk. A dough around 78–80°F will move far faster than one at 70°F.

Frequently asked questions

What does overproofed sourdough look like?

The dough is very puffy, slack and sticky, may have big bubbles on the surface, and spreads or collapses when turned out or scored. The baked loaf is flat with little oven spring.

Can you fix overproofed sourdough dough?

Often. Gently reshape it and give it a short second proof, bake it in a loaf pan, or turn it into focaccia.

How do I know if my sourdough is overproofed or underproofed?

Overproofed loaves are flat and wide with large holes near the top; underproofed loaves are tight and dense, often with a few big tunnels. The poke test before baking helps tell them apart.

Can you bake overproofed sourdough?

Yes. It will be flatter, but it is safe to eat and usually still tastes good.

How do I stop my sourdough overproofing?

Shorten bulk fermentation in warm weather, use less starter, judge the rise instead of the clock, and proof the shaped loaf in the fridge.
