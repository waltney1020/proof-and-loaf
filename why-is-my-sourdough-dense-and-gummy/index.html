Why Is My Sourdough Dense and Gummy? 9 Causes and Fixes

A heavy, tight or gummy loaf is one of the most common sourdough letdowns. The good news is that it nearly always comes down to time, temperature or patience, not your recipe or your gear.

Quick answer

  • Dense and tight usually means the dough was underproofed or the starter wasn’t at its peak when you mixed. In a cool kitchen, bulk fermentation simply needs more time.
  • Gummy or wet inside usually means the loaf was underbaked or sliced too soon. Bake until the crust is deep brown and let it cool for at least an hour before cutting.
  • Both at once often points to a sluggish starter plus a cold kitchen, or a dough that’s wetter than your flour can handle.
  • An instant-read thermometer answers two of the biggest questions for very little money: how warm is your dough, and is the bread baked through?

Dense vs gummy: what each one tells you

People often say “dense and gummy” as if it’s one problem, but they usually have different causes. A dense loaf has small, tight holes and feels heavy for its size; that’s mostly a fermentation problem. A gummy crumb looks damp or translucent and sticks to the knife; that’s mostly a baking or cooling problem. Look at a slice and decide which you have before changing anything.

9 common causes and what to try

1. Bulk fermentation was too short

This is the most common cause of a dense loaf. Recipes give times that assume a warm kitchen, often around 75–78°F (24–26°C). In a 68°F kitchen the same dough can need several more hours. Go by the dough instead of the clock: it should look noticeably bigger (many bakers aim for roughly 50–75% rise), feel airy and jiggly, show bubbles on the sides and top, and have a slightly domed surface. A straight-sided container with the starting level marked makes the rise easy to judge.

2. The starter wasn’t at its peak

Mixing with a starter that hasn’t risen yet, or that peaked and collapsed hours ago, gives the dough a slow start. Use it when it’s domed or just starting to flatten, with bubbles throughout. If your starter is unpredictable, fix that first: our starter not rising guide has a simple reset plan.

3. Your kitchen is cold

Cooler weather slows everything down. Free fixes: lukewarm mixing water, the oven with only the light on (check it with a thermometer first and leave a note so nobody preheats it), or the top of the fridge. A folding proofer holds a set temperature if your kitchen is cold for months; our proofer and starter warmer guide compares it with the free options.

4. Weak shaping

A loose, slack shape spreads instead of rising, giving a flat, tight loaf. Build surface tension when you shape so the dough holds itself up, and use a banneton or a towel-lined bowl so it keeps its shape in the final proof.

5. Too much water for your flour

High-hydration recipes look great online but are hard to handle with all-purpose flour or as a beginner. Too-wet dough can bake up gummy and flat. Try holding back 25–50 g of the water next time and adding it only if the dough feels stiff.

6. Too much whole grain or rye, too soon

Whole wheat and rye make wonderful bread but give a naturally denser, moister crumb. If your recipe uses a lot, that may be normal. Start with 10–20% whole grain and increase once you’re happy with the result.

7. Underbaking

Pale bread is often gummy bread. Bake until the crust is a deep golden brown. Many bakers check that the inside has reached roughly 205–210°F (96–99°C). If the crust is browning too fast, lower the heat slightly for the last stretch rather than pulling the loaf early.

8. Slicing it too soon

The crumb keeps setting as the loaf cools, and steam is still escaping. Cutting into a hot loaf can make perfectly baked bread seem gummy. Let it cool on a rack for at least an hour, ideally two.

9. Not enough heat or steam at the start

Without a hot, steamy start, the crust sets before the loaf finishes rising in the oven, so it stays tighter. A preheated Dutch oven or combo cooker traps the loaf’s own steam. No pot? Our no-Dutch-oven guide covers free alternatives.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my sourdough dense?

Most often the dough was underproofed: bulk fermentation ended too soon, usually because the kitchen was cool or the starter wasn't at its peak. Let the dough rise by roughly 50 to 75% and look airy before shaping.

Why is my sourdough gummy inside?

Usually it was underbaked or sliced while still hot. Bake until the crust is deep brown, and let the loaf cool for at least an hour before cutting.

Can overproofing make sourdough gummy?

Yes. An overproofed dough can collapse and bake into a flat loaf with a dense, gummy layer near the bottom, so check the other signs before deciding which problem you have.

What internal temperature should sourdough be?

Many bakers aim for roughly 205 to 210°F (96 to 99°C) in the center, along with a deep brown crust.

Is my sourdough undercooked if it's gummy?

It may be, but cutting it too soon causes the same look. If a fully cooled loaf is still gummy, bake longer next time.
