You can score a loaf with almost anything sharp. Here’s how a lame, a bare razor blade and a kitchen knife actually compare, and when each is worth using.

Quick answer

  • Best overall: a bread lame. It’s a razor blade on a handle, so you get the same clean cut with more control and your fingers away from the edge.
  • Cheapest that works well: a bare double-edge razor blade, held very carefully, or slipped onto a coffee stirrer.
  • In a pinch: a very sharp, thin knife or serrated tomato knife. Expect more dragging on wet dough.
  • Avoid: dull kitchen knives. Box cutters can work but have thicker blades that cut less cleanly.

Bread lame vs razor blade

The blade is the same: most lames take standard double-edge razor blades. The difference is the handle. A lame holds the blade at a consistent angle, lets you curve it for an “ear,” and keeps your fingertips away from the edge. A bare blade cuts just as cleanly, but it’s small, slippery with flour, and sharp on both sides, which makes long confident strokes harder and accidents more likely. A popular hack is to thread a blade onto a wooden coffee stirrer for a makeshift lame.

Bread lame vs knife

Even sharp kitchen knives have a thicker edge than a razor blade. On sticky, high-hydration dough the blade tends to drag, pulling the skin instead of slicing it, so the score seals up or tears. A knife works better on firm, cold dough straight from the fridge. If you use one, pick the thinnest, sharpest blade you own, and make one quick stroke rather than sawing. A serrated tomato knife can grip the surface better than a dull straight edge.

Bread lame vs box cutter

A utility knife blade is sharp and has a handle, so it’s a reasonable stand-in. The blade is thicker than a razor blade, so cuts can be slightly less clean, and it should be a fresh blade you keep only for food. Don’t use one that has been cutting cardboard or tape.

Our take

If you bake more than a few loaves a year, a lame is one of the least expensive tools that makes a visible difference. Try a bare blade or a knife first if you want, but most bakers find the control of a handle worth it.

Scoring tips that matter more than the tool

  • Score cold dough. Dough from an overnight fridge proof is firmer and scores far more cleanly with any tool.
  • Dust the top. A light dusting of rice flour shows your scores and reduces drag.
  • One confident stroke. Hesitant, repeated cuts tear the skin.
  • Shallow angle for an ear. Hold the blade at roughly 30° to the surface for one long cut, about a quarter to half an inch deep.
  • Dispose of blades safely. Keep used blades in a lidded tin or blade bank, not loose in the trash.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bread lame better than a razor blade?

The cut is the same, since most lames hold a standard razor blade. The handle gives a steadier angle, lets you curve the blade, and keeps fingers away from the edge.

Can I score sourdough with a knife?

Yes, in a pinch. Use the thinnest, sharpest knife you have, score cold dough, and make one quick stroke. Expect more drag than with a razor blade.

Can I use a box cutter to score bread?

A fresh utility blade kept only for food can work. It is thicker than a razor blade, so cuts may be slightly less clean.

What blades go in a bread lame?

Most lames take standard double-edge safety razor blades. Check your lame's instructions before buying replacements.

What is the best bread lame alternative?

A double-edge razor blade threaded onto a wooden coffee stirrer is the classic homemade lame. Kitchen scissors work for decorative snips.
