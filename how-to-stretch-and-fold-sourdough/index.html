Quick answer

  • What it is: a gentle way to strengthen dough by hand during bulk fermentation, instead of long kneading.
  • Typical rhythm: many bakers do 3–4 sets, spaced about 20–40 minutes apart, in the first couple of hours of bulk.
  • Stop when the dough feels stronger: it should hold shape better, look smoother and pass a short windowpane or “jiggle” check.
  • Wet hands, not flour: wet hands keep sticky dough from clinging without drying it out.

Why stretch and fold works

Sourdough dough is usually too wet and sticky for classic kneading on the counter. Stretch-and-folds (and coil folds) build gluten a little at a time while the dough rests. Each set lines up the gluten strands, traps gas and helps the dough hold its shape later when you shape and bake. You spend a minute or two every half hour instead of ten minutes of kneading up front.

These folds happen during bulk fermentation. Bulk starts when the dough is mixed; the folds are usually front-loaded in that first 1–2 hours while the dough is still extensible.

How to stretch and fold, step by step

  1. Mix the dough until there is no dry flour. Many bakers rest it 20–40 minutes (an autolyse or a short rest after mixing in the starter and salt) before the first fold.
  2. Wet your hands so the dough doesn’t stick.
  3. Slide one hand under a side of the dough, stretch it up gently, and fold it over the center.
  4. Turn the bowl a quarter turn and repeat on each side (usually 4 folds = one “set”).
  5. Cover the bowl and rest 20–40 minutes.
  6. Repeat for 3–4 sets, then leave the dough alone to finish bulk until it has risen about 50–75% and looks airy.

Coil folds are a gentler cousin: lift the dough from the middle with both hands and let the ends tuck under. Use whatever your recipe suggests; the goal is the same.

How many sets, and when to stop

Dough feelWhat to do
Slack, tears easily, spreads in the bowlKeep folding on schedule; it needs more strength.
Smooth, holds a dome, jiggles as one pieceYou can stop folding and let bulk finish undisturbed.
Tight, tears when you stretchBe gentler or skip the next set; the gluten is already strong.

A common beginner schedule is four sets at 30-minute intervals, then rest. Higher-hydration doughs and whole-grain doughs often need the full set of folds; stiffer white doughs may need fewer.

Common stretch-and-fold mistakes

  • Dusting with lots of flour. That dries the dough. Wet hands instead.
  • Ripping the dough. Stretch only as far as it will go without tearing; wait a few minutes if it fights you.
  • Folding all the way through bulk. Late folds can degas a dough that is already airy. Front-load them.
  • Judging readiness only by the clock. Temperature changes bulk time; see our bulk fermentation time guide.
  • Under-fermenting after the folds. Strong dough still needs enough rise. Ending bulk too early is a top cause of dense, gummy sourdough.
  • Overproofing after a warm bulk. If the dough went too far, read overproofed signs and fixes.

Stretch and fold vs. kneading vs. a mixer

You do not need a stand mixer for everyday sourdough. Hand folds are the default in most beginner recipes. A mixer can develop gluten faster with a dough hook, but it is easy to overwork a wet dough, and it is one more machine to store. Kneading on the counter works better for lower-hydration doughs; for typical sourdough, folds are cleaner.

Frequently asked questions

How many stretch and folds does sourdough need?

Many beginner recipes use about 3 to 4 sets, spaced 20 to 40 minutes apart, in the first 1 to 2 hours of bulk fermentation. Stop earlier if the dough already feels strong and smooth.

When should I start stretch and folds?

After mixing (and often after a short rest), once there is no dry flour left. Bulk fermentation has already started; the folds are part of that first stage.

Should I use wet hands or flour?

Wet hands. Extra flour dries the dough and changes hydration. A quick rinse between folds is usually enough.

What is the difference between stretch and fold and coil fold?

Both build strength. Stretch-and-fold pulls one side up and over; coil fold lifts from the middle and tucks the ends under. Use whichever your recipe prefers.

Do I need a stand mixer if I stretch and fold?

No. Hand folds are the standard method in most home sourdough recipes. A mixer is optional, not required.
